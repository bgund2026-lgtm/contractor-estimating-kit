# The Contractor's Pricing Reference Guide

**How to price jobs so you actually make money — not just cover costs.**

## 1. How to Calculate Your Real Hourly Rate

Most contractors price by the job, not the hour. But you can't price a job correctly if you don't know what your time is worth. Here's the real formula.

### The Formula

```
Your Hourly Rate = (Annual Income Goal + Annual Overhead + Annual Benefits)
                  ÷
                  Billable Hours Per Year
```

### Breaking It Down

**Annual Income Goal:** What you want to take home. Be honest — not what you think you should want, but what you actually need to live the life you want. $75K? $120K? $200K? Pick a number.

**Annual Overhead:** Every dollar your business spends that isn't on a job site:

| Expense | Monthly | Annual |
|---------|---------|--------|
| Truck payment + insurance | $1,200 | $14,400 |
| Tool replacement/maintenance | $300 | $3,600 |
| Office (home office or rent) | $500 | $6,000 |
| Phone + internet | $150 | $1,800 |
| Accounting/bookkeeping | $300 | $3,600 |
| Liability insurance | $350 | $4,200 |
| WCB/WSIB premiums | $400 | $4,800 |
| Software (estimating, CRM, etc.) | $100 | $1,200 |
| Marketing (website, ads, cards) | $300 | $3,600 |
| Continuing education/licenses | $100 | $1,200 |
| Office supplies | $75 | $900 |
| **Total Overhead** | **$3,775** | **$45,300** |

**Annual Benefits:** What you'd get at a regular job that you're paying out of pocket:

| Benefit | Annual |
|---------|--------|
| Health/dental insurance | $6,000 |
| Retirement contribution (RRSP/401k) | $10,000 |
| Vacation pay (4 weeks @ your rate) | $8,000 |
| Sick days (5 days) | $1,500 |
| **Total Benefits** | **$25,500** |

**Billable Hours Per Year:** This is where most contractors go wrong. You do NOT bill 2,000 hours a year.

| | Hours |
|---|-------|
| Total weeks per year | 52 |
| Minus: Vacation (4 weeks) | -4 |
| Minus: Stats/holidays (10 days) | -2 |
| Minus: Sick time (5 days) | -1 |
| **Working weeks** | **45** |
| **Working days** | **225** |
| Hours per day | 8 |
| **Total working hours** | **1,800** |
| Minus: Non-billable (estimating, admin, calls, driving, picking materials) | -30% |
| **Billable hours** | **1,260** |

That's right. Out of 2,000 possible hours, you'll actually bill about 1,260. The other 540 are spent on things you can't bill but have to do anyway.

### The Calculation — Example

Let's say you want to take home $100,000:

```
Hourly Rate = ($100,000 + $45,300 + $25,500) ÷ 1,260
            = $170,800 ÷ 1,260
            = $135.56/hour
```

**Your real hourly rate is $135.56.** That's what an hour of your time on a job site costs your business. If you charge less, you're subsidizing clients with your own quality of life.

**Round up:** In this example, you'd charge $140/hour minimum. Some contractors use $150. That buffer covers the unexpected.

### Quick Reference Table

| Desired Take-Home | Your Rate (at $45K overhead, $25K benefits) |
|-------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| $60,000 | $103/hr |
| $80,000 | $119/hr |
| $100,000 | $135/hr |
| $120,000 | $151/hr |
| $150,000 | $175/hr |

*Adjust overhead and benefits to your actual numbers. These are averages for a solo contractor with a truck and basic setup.*

## 2. Markup vs. Margin — The Difference That Costs You Thousands

**This is the #1 pricing mistake contractors make.** It sounds like semantics. It's not.

### Markup

Markup is what you ADD to your cost to get your price.

```
Selling Price = Cost × (1 + Markup%)
```

Example: Material costs $100. You mark it up 30%.
- Selling price = $100 × 1.30 = **$130**

### Margin

Margin is the PERCENTAGE of the selling price that's profit.

```
Margin% = (Selling Price - Cost) ÷ Selling Price × 100
```

Example: You sell something for $130 that cost you $100.
- Margin = ($130 - $100) ÷ $130 × 100 = **23.1%**

**Notice: A 30% markup gives you a 23.1% margin, NOT a 30% margin.**

### Why This Matters

If you WANT a 30% profit margin, here's what you actually need to charge:

```
Selling Price = Cost ÷ (1 - Margin%)
Selling Price = $100 ÷ (1 - 0.30) = $142.86
```

**At a 30% markup, you make $30 on a $130 sale (23.1% margin).**
**At a 30% margin, you make $42.86 on a $142.86 sale (30% margin).**

Over a year, that difference is thousands of dollars.

### Quick Conversion Table

| If you want this margin | You need this markup |
|------------------------|---------------------|
| 10% | 11.1% |
| 15% | 17.6% |
| 20% | 25.0% |
| 25% | 33.3% |
| 30% | 42.9% |
| 35% | 53.8% |
| 40% | 66.7% |
| 50% | 100.0% |

**Rule of thumb:** If you want X% margin, your markup needs to be HIGHER than X%. Always.

## 3. Material Markup Strategies

### When to Markup

**Always.** Every piece of material that passes through your hands has a cost to your business — the time to source it, order it, pick it up (or receive it), verify it, store it, and deliver it to the site. That cost deserves to be paid.

The question isn't "should I markup materials?" The question is "how much?"

### How Much to Markup

| Category | Recommended Markup | Why |
|----------|-------------------|-----|
| Standard materials (lumber, drywall, wire, pipe) | 20-30% | You're ordering, picking up, delivering, and managing returns |
| Specialty items (custom fixtures, special-order tile) | 15-20% | Less handling, but you're fronting the money and managing the order |
| Large equipment (furnace, AC, water heater) | 10-15% | Lower handling, but warranty support and coordination add value |
| Consumables (screws, tape, glue, blades) | 50-100% | You can't track every box of screws — bundle them into your overhead or markup them high |
| Client-requested upgrades | 25-35% | They chose the expensive option — the extra coordination has a price |

### The "I Get Contractor Pricing" Client

Some clients will ask you to pass through your trade discount. Here's how to handle it:

**Option 1 (Recommended):** "I price materials at retail. My trade discount covers the time I spend sourcing, ordering, coordinating delivery, verifying quantities, and handling returns/warranties. You're paying for that convenience, not just the material."

**Option 2:** If the client insists on cost-plus, charge a 25-35% handling fee ON TOP of your actual cost. You still get paid for your time; they see the "discount."

**Option 3:** Client buys all materials themselves. Fine — but make it clear: you don't warranty materials you didn't source, and delays from missing/wrong materials are on their timeline, not yours.

## 5. How to Price Emergency & After-Hours Work

### The Reality

You're getting called at 9 PM on a Saturday because a pipe burst. That call has a price — and it should be significantly higher than your weekday rate. Not because you're greedy, but because:

1. You're dropping everything — including your personal life
2. You may not have your full crew or materials available
3. After-hours work is harder, slower, and more prone to issues
4. You're solving an urgent problem — urgency has value

### Recommended After-Hours Pricing

| Time | Multiplier | Example ($140/hr base) |
|------|-----------|----------------------|
| Weekday evenings (after 6 PM) | 1.5× | $210/hr |
| Weekends (Sat–Sun, regular hours) | 1.5× | $210/hr |
| Weekend evenings | 2.0× | $280/hr |
| Statutory holidays | 2.0× | $280/hr |
| Emergency call-outs (within 2 hours) | 2.0× | $280/hr |
| Middle-of-the-night emergency (11 PM–6 AM) | 2.5× | $350/hr |

### Minimum Charges

- **Emergency call-out minimum:** 2 hours (even if the fix takes 20 minutes). You're not just billing for the work — you're billing for the mobilization, the interruption, and the availability.
- **Weekend work minimum:** 4 hours. Half your Saturday is gone whether the job takes 2 hours or 6.

### How to Communicate This

> "I can absolutely come out tonight. My emergency rate is $[X]/hour with a 2-hour minimum. I'll have someone there by [time]. Should I head over?"

Don't apologize for the rate. Don't explain it unless they ask. State it clearly and let them decide if the emergency is worth the price. Most of the time, it is.

## 6. Change Order Pricing — Why 1.5× to 2× Is Fair

### The Math Behind the Multiplier

Change orders cost more than the same work would cost if it was in the original scope. Here's why:

**1. Context Switching:** You've got a schedule, a crew, and a workflow. A change order pulls you out of that rhythm. Even a "quick" addition means 30 minutes of context-switching — re-reading the scope, figuring out where the new work fits, adjusting the schedule, communicating with the crew. That's 30 minutes of non-productive time you don't have on planned work.

**2. Schedule Disruption:** The change order doesn't just add work — it delays everything after it. If you're adding 2 days of work in the middle of a 3-week project, the remaining phases shift by 2 days. That means the client's other contractors, the inspection schedule, and the final completion date all move. Managing that ripple is work.

**3. Material Sourcing:** Original-scope materials were ordered in advance at good prices. Change order materials are often rush-ordered, which means premium pricing and expedited shipping.

**4. Mental Load:** Every change order requires you to estimate, present, get approval, schedule, execute, and document. That's easily 2-3 hours of admin work per change order, regardless of the size of the actual work.

**5. Risk:** Change orders are inherently riskier than planned work. You're working around completed work, integrating with existing systems, and often dealing with unforeseen conditions. More risk = higher price.

### The Standard

**1.5× your standard rate** for change orders that are requested during the project but don't require schedule gymnastics.

**2.0× your standard rate** for change orders that require rush work, after-hours work, or significant schedule reshuffling.

### Example

Standard labor rate: $55/hour
Change order rate (1.5×): $82.50/hour

A change order for 8 hours of additional tile work:
- At standard rate: $440
- At change order rate: $660
- Difference: $220

That $220 covers the 30 minutes of context-switching, 2 hours of admin, the schedule adjustment, and the risk of working around finished areas. It's not a penalty — it's the real cost.

## 7. Common Estimating Mistakes (And How to Avoid Them)

### Mistake 1: Estimating by Square Footage Alone

**What you do:** "Bathrooms are $200/sq ft, so an 80 sq ft bathroom is $16,000."

**Why it fails:** A 5×8 bathroom with a toilet, sink, and tub surround is different from a 5×8 bathroom with a walk-in shower, heated floor, custom vanity, and double sinks. Same square footage, wildly different costs.

**The fix:** Estimate by task, not by area. Break the job into phases (demo, plumbing, electrical, tile, fixtures, paint) and price each based on what's actually involved.

### Mistake 2: Forgetting the "Invisible" Work

**What you forget:**
- Hauling and disposal (dumpster rental, dump fees, gas, time)
- Permits and inspections
- Temporary utilities (if water/power is off during work)
- Dust control and protection of client's property
- Daily clean-up time (30 min–1 hour per day)
- Final clean (4-8 hours on a typical remodel)
- Touch-up work after other trades
- Coordination and scheduling time (your time as PM)
- Warranty service calls

**The fix:** Add a line item for "project management and clean-up" — 10-15% of total labor. Or build it into your overhead.

### Mistake 3: Underestimating by 20%

**The research:** Multiple studies show that contractors consistently underestimate project costs by 15-30%. It's not dishonesty — it's optimism. You imagine everything going smoothly, and it never does.

**The fix:** After you finish your estimate, add 15-20% contingency. Not as a line item the client sees — as a buffer in your pricing. If the job should cost $20,000, quote $23,000-24,000. If everything goes perfectly, you make more margin. If things go sideways (they will), you're covered.

### Mistake 4: Not Accounting for Waste

**What you do:** Calculate exactly 85 sq ft of tile for an 85 sq ft bathroom floor. Order 85 sq ft.

**Why it fails:** Cuts, breaks, mistakes, and pattern matching mean you need extra. Tile is 10-15% waste. Lumber is 10%. Drywall is 5%. Sheet goods are 10-15%.

**The fix:** Add waste factors to EVERY material:
- Tile (straight lay): 10%
- Tile (diagonal or pattern): 15-20%
- Lumber: 10%
- Drywall: 5-10%
- Insulation: 5-10%
- Wire/cable: 10%
- Paint: 5-10%

Build waste into your material takeoff, not as a separate line.

### Mistake 5: Using Last Year's Prices

**What you do:** Use the same unit costs you used 18 months ago because "that's what it costs."

**Why it fails:** Material prices change — sometimes dramatically. Lumber went up 200% in 2021. Copper fluctuates constantly. Labor rates increase 3-5% per year.

**The fix:** Get fresh quotes for every job. Don't estimate a material cost you haven't verified in the last 90 days. The estimating spreadsheet reference data in this kit is a starting point — not a substitute for a real quote.

### Mistake 6: Pricing to Win Instead of Pricing to Profit

**What you do:** You know the job should be $25,000 but you "know the client won't pay that" so you quote $18,000. You win the job. You lose money.

**Why it fails:** The client's budget is the client's problem, not yours. If the job costs $25,000 to do right, that's the price. If you do it for $18,000, you'll cut corners, lose money, and resent the project.

**The fix:** Price every job to make your target margin. If the client can't afford it, offer alternatives — reduce the scope, change the materials, phase the project. Never reduce your price without reducing the scope.

### Mistake 7: Not Tracking Actual vs. Estimated

**What you do:** Finish a job, move to the next one, never look back.

**Why it fails:** If you don't track what jobs actually cost you, you can't improve your estimating. Every job should be a data point that makes your next estimate better.

**The fix:** After every job, sit down with your estimate and your actuals. Where were you over? Where were you under? What did you forget? What took longer than expected? Update your reference data. Your estimating gets more accurate over time — but only if you track it.
